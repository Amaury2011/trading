The Open — How Stocks Work

Before you trade anything, it helps to know what you actually own and where the price you see is coming from.

Ownership

What a share actually is

A share is a small legal slice of a company. Own one, and you own a fraction of its assets, earnings, and voting rights. A company sells shares to raise money without taking on debt — investors buy them expecting the company to grow in value or pay out profit as dividends.

Marketplace

Where trades happen

Exchanges like the NYSE or Nasdaq are matching engines — they connect a buyer's bid with a seller's ask. Your broker routes your order there electronically; the whole match usually happens in milliseconds.

  • Market Cap
    Share price × total shares outstanding. It's the market's running estimate of what the whole company is worth — not the same as cash in the bank.
  • IPO
    The Initial Public Offering — the first time a private company sells shares to the public and starts trading on an exchange.
  • Index
    A basket of stocks tracked together, like the S&P 500. Indices are used as a scoreboard for "the market" as a whole, and as a benchmark to compare your own returns against.
  • Bull / Bear
    A bull market is a sustained period of rising prices and optimism. A bear market is the opposite — a sustained decline, usually 20%+ off recent highs, paired with pessimism.
  • Volatility
    How much and how fast a price moves. High volatility means bigger swings in both directions — more opportunity, and more risk, in the same breath.

    The Order — How Trading Works

    Every trade is just two people disagreeing on price until they don't. Here's the mechanics of getting your order filled.

    Order types

    Speed over price

    Market order

    Buy or sell immediately at the best available current price. You're guaranteed to get filled, not guaranteed the exact price.

    Price over speed

    Limit order

    You set the exact price you're willing to buy or sell at. It only fills if the market reaches that price — you're guaranteed the price, not guaranteed a fill.

  • Long position
    Buying a stock expecting the price to rise, then selling later for a profit. The default, simplest position.
  • Short position
    Borrowing shares to sell now, aiming to buy them back cheaper later. Profits if the price falls — but losses are theoretically unlimited, since a price has no ceiling.
  • Stop-loss
    A standing order that automatically sells your position if the price drops to a level you set — a pre-committed exit that limits how much a single trade can cost you.
  • Position size
    How much of your account you put into one trade. Risking a small, consistent slice per trade (many traders use 1–2%) is what keeps one bad trade from being a account-ending one.

    The Candle — Reading Price Action

    A candlestick compresses four numbers — open, high, low, close — into one shape. Learn the shapes, and a chart starts reading like a sentence.

    Anatomy

    Body and wick

    The thick body spans the open and close for that period. The thin wick (or shadow) marks the high and low reached along the way. A green (or hollow) body means the close was above the open; a red (or filled) body means the close was below it.

    Reading it

    One candle tells a mood

    A long body means conviction — one side dominated the period. A long wick with a small body means rejection — price tried to move that way and got pushed back. No wick at all means one side controlled the entire range.

    Doji Indecision

    Open and close land almost at the same price. Long wicks on both sides show a real fight between buyers and sellers that ended in a draw — often a sign momentum is stalling.

    Hammer Bullish reversal

    Small body near the top, long lower wick, little to no upper wick. Sellers pushed price down hard, but buyers stepped in and dragged it back up before the close. Found at the bottom of a downtrend.

    Shooting Star Bearish reversal

    The mirror image of a hammer: small body near the bottom, long upper wick. Buyers tried to push price up, sellers overpowered them by the close. Found at the top of an uptrend.

    Marubozu Strong conviction

    No wicks at all — the open was the low and the close was the high (or vice versa for a bearish marubozu). One side controlled the entire period, start to finish.

    Bullish Engulfing Bullish reversal

    A small red candle is followed by a larger green candle whose body fully swallows the one before it. Buyers didn't just stop the decline — they erased it in a single period.

    Bearish Engulfing Bearish reversal

    The opposite setup at the top of an uptrend: a small green candle, then a larger red one that fully engulfs it. Sellers take back control in one decisive move.

    Morning Star Bullish reversal

    Three candles: a long decline, a small indecisive candle that gaps lower, then a strong green candle that climbs back into the first candle's body. The "star" in the middle marks the turn.

    Evening Star Bearish reversal

    The mirror of a morning star: a strong rally, a small indecisive candle at the top, then a long red candle erasing much of the gain. Marks exhaustion at the top of a trend.

    Three White Soldiers Bullish continuation

    Three consecutive long green candles, each opening inside the previous body and closing higher, with small wicks throughout. Steady, sustained buying pressure — no single burst.

    The Strategy — Putting It Together

    A strategy is just a rule set: what setup you look for, when you enter, and when you get out. Here are four common frameworks traders build on top of price action.

    Trend following

    TOOLS: MOVING AVERAGES

    Trade in the direction of the prevailing trend rather than against it — "the trend is your friend." A common trigger is a shorter moving average (e.g. 20-day) crossing above a longer one (e.g. 50-day), used as a signal that momentum has shifted.

    Breakout trading

    TOOLS: SUPPORT / RESISTANCE

    Watch a price that's been trapped between a floor (support) and a ceiling (resistance), and enter when it breaks convincingly through one of those levels, betting the move continues in that direction.

    Mean reversion

    TOOLS: RSI, BOLLINGER BANDS

    Based on the idea that price tends to snap back toward its average after moving too far, too fast. Traders often use the RSI (Relative Strength Index) to flag when a stock looks "overbought" or "oversold."

    Candlestick confirmation

    TOOLS: MODULE 03 PATTERNS

    Rather than a standalone strategy, this is a filter layered onto the others — e.g. only taking a breakout trade if it closes as a marubozu, or only trusting a support bounce if it prints a hammer.

    Non-negotiable

    Risk management outranks the strategy

    Every strategy above will lose some percentage of the time — that's normal, not a sign you did it wrong. What separates a sustainable trader from a blown-up account is deciding your stop-loss and position size before you enter, not after the trade has already moved against you.
